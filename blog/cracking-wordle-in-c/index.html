Cracking Wordle in C

2 Nov 2026

Wordle is a solved game, in the strict sense: somebody has computed the globally optimal policy and proved that it's optimal. So writing a solver should be a weekend of transcription, and that's what I expected going in.

It mostly was. The part I didn't expect was that the hardest problem had nothing to do with the search.

What was already solved

Dimitris Bertsimas and Alex Paskov published An Exact Solution to Wordle in Operations Research (73(3):1384-1394, 2025). They model the game as a Markov decision process, write down the Bellman equation, and solve it by exact dynamic programming. The result is that SALET is the optimal opening word, the optimal policy averages 3.4212 guesses, and it never needs more than five. Alex Selby reached the same answer independently by exhaustive search in January 2022.

I should be clear that this is the part of my program that works best, and none of it is mine. The recurrence my solver computes is their Bellman equation. On the canonical lists (2315 answers plus 10657 extra legal guesses, 12972 total) my C version returns 7920 total guesses, 3.4212 average, worst case 5. That isn't an improvement on the paper. It's the ceiling the paper proved, and matching it exactly is the best available outcome.

I take salet on faith too. Deriving the opener needs a full-width search over all 12972 possible first guesses, which is hours of compute, not milliseconds. Every guess after the first one is searched for real at runtime.

Encoding the game as bytes

A Wordle clue is five trits (grey 0, yellow 1, green 2), so there are 3^5 = 243 possible colourings and all of them fit in a byte. Pack it base-3 and the game becomes integer arithmetic:

/* colour of `answer` when you guess `guess`, one byte; 242 == all green */
uint8_t PAT [NGUESS][NANS];    /* 12972 x  2315  =  30 MB */
uint8_t PATW[NGUESS][NGUESS];  /* 12972 x 12972  = 168 MB */

Both tables get built once across threads and then mmap'd from a cache directory, keyed by an FNV hash of the word list, so changing the words rebuilds them automatically. After that, scoring a guess against a candidate is a single array load. Nothing in the hot path touches a string.

Filtering is just as crude. When you tell the solver ybbbg, that parses to a byte, and it keeps the candidates whose table entry equals that byte. There's no per-letter constraint bookkeeping anywhere, and the duplicate-letter cases come out right for free, because the table was built with the real rule: a green claims the answer's copy of a letter first, and only leftover copies can turn a later repeat yellow. So geese against these is bbggg, not ygggg. A lot of solvers get this wrong, and precomputing it means you can only get it wrong once.

The search is the Bellman recurrence with the usual load-bearing tricks. A 2n-1 lower bound on any candidate set, since you pay one guess per candidate plus a second for all but the one you might luckily name this turn. Alpha/beta-style budget propagation across sibling buckets. A Zobrist-hashed transposition table over candidate sets. A beam over a cheap one-ply ranking, wide enough that from turn two onward the result is the true optimum.

It worked, it matched the paper, and I thought I was done.

The game it got wrong

I played a real puzzle through it. It opened salet, I fed it colours, it narrowed down to one candidate and guessed wrist. I entered bgggg, meaning the first letter was wrong and the rest were correct, and it said:

those colours rule out every word, they can't all be right.

I had entered everything correctly. The answer was grist. My solver was telling me a true thing was impossible, with complete confidence, because grist wasn't in its answer list.

That's the whole project in one error message. The 2315-word list was accurate for Wordle as it existed when the list was scraped, and then the New York Times bought the game and started editing the answers. The proof is still a proof. It's just a proof about a slightly different game than the one on my screen.

The failure mode is what bothers me. A solver that treats "not in my list" as "impossible" doesn't degrade gracefully when the list drifts. It becomes confidently wrong, and it sounds exactly as sure of itself as it did when it was right.

A prior instead of a list

The fix is to stop pretending I know the answer set. Rather than a binary possible/impossible, every word carries a prior weight, and the objective becomes

minimize  sum over words of  weight(word) x guesses-to-find-it

where --prior P is P(the answer is on the official list) and derives the weight ratio between list words and the other 10657. At --prior 1 the extras weigh nothing and you recover the paper's problem exactly, which is useful: it means every later change can be checked against a certified number. At the default of 0.95 the extras stay alive as candidates, just cheap. Nothing legal is ever pruned, so the contradiction I hit can't happen at all, rather than merely being unlikely.

That created a second problem, which turned out to be more interesting than the first.

Pricing a loss

With all 12972 words alive, some positions genuinely can't be won in the turns remaining. My search reported those as INF, infeasible. That's true and completely useless, because once a value is infinite every losing line looks exactly as bad as every other losing line. The search couldn't tell them apart, so it threw away the whole six-guess budget and started losing games it could have won.

So I stopped calling it infeasible and charged for it instead. A candidate you fail to pin down costs LOSS = 16 guess-equivalents per unit of weight. Now the search can see that one disaster is worse than another, and it does what I wanted without being told: it sacrifices improbable words to keep likely ones inside six guesses. It chooses which games to lose.

I tried LOSS at 48 and at 128. Both were worse. Too large a penalty and the lower bound stops pruning effectively, so the search gets slower and plays worse at the same time. 16 is what I settled on. At --prior 1 nothing is ever over budget, so the term never fires and the certified result is untouched.

Something smaller fell out of the same thinking. On the last allowed turn, a clever splitting guess wins with probability zero, so however bad the position is you should name the likeliest remaining candidate and take the chance. My solver had been playing the splitter. That was free wins I'd been giving away by not noticing an obvious thing.

What it costs

answer list: avgworstall 12972: avgunsolved
--prior 1 (the paper)3.421254.3585117 (0.90%)
--prior 0.95 (default)3.423354.269637 (0.29%)
flat prior3.781964.06601 (0.01%)

The default gives up 0.0021 guesses of average performance on the official list, keeps the never-more-than-five guarantee, and cuts failures on arbitrary English words by two thirds. Stated plainly: my default is worse than the paper on the paper's own metric, and better on an axis the paper doesn't measure. That's a reasonable trade when the answer list is a moving target, and a bad one if you trust the list.

None of this is novel. 3Blue1Brown weighted words by frequency years ago, just paired with an entropy heuristic rather than exact DP, and replacing a hard constraint with a penalty is a first-week trick. I'd assume the combination has been written up somewhere I haven't read.

The bug Python found

This is the part I'd most want someone else to take away.

In hard mode, where every guess has to be consistent with all the clues so far, the solver reported that vaunt took seven guesses. It walks daunt, gaunt, haunt, jaunt one at a time instead of playing a splitter that separates them in a single move. I looked at that, reasoned about the constraints, concluded the position was genuinely unwinnable, and said so out loud with some confidence.

Then I wrote an independent brute force in Python, from scratch, sharing no code with the C version, and it found a six-guess line.

Two real bugs, both of which had been there the entire time:

  • The per-guess evaluator passed its children the parent's legal-guess pool in hard mode instead of narrowing it. So it valued every guess as though later turns were unconstrained, which rates losing lines as winning ones. vaunt went from 7 to 4 once I fixed it.
  • The transposition table keyed only on the candidate set. In hard mode the legal-guess pool is part of the position, because the same set of surviving candidates reached through different clue histories has a different optimal value once your future guesses are constrained. That one looked harmless right up until I started writing those values to disk.

Both were invisible at --prior 1. My headline benchmark, the one that matched a certified published result to four decimal places, exercised neither of them. I had the most reassuring evidence of correctness available and two live bugs sitting behind it.

I also had to retract a number I'd already stated. I measured hard mode in an intermediate state, with one bug fixed and the other still there, decided the previously documented figure had been an artifact, and announced the new one as the honest value. The original was right. Reproducing a published optimum tells you one configuration is correct and nothing at all about the others, and I'd been treating it as a character reference for the whole program.

Write the second implementation. Not a second test, a second implementation, in a different language, ideally without reference to how you did it the first time. It's the only thing that caught this.

Where it still loses

Thirty-seven words out of 12972 still go unsolved, and the list is embarrassing: fixed and fazed sit in it next to fuffy, poovy and zills. The reason is that my prior is uniform across all 10657 non-list words, so the solver can't tell fixed from fuffy, and when it decides which games to sacrifice it's spending the budget blind.

I tried to fix this with a plausibility model built from letter and bigram statistics over the answer list, and then threw it out. It put fixed in the 4.7th percentile, because x is rare and an ordinary word ends up looking like noise, and crore in the 99.8th. It measures orthographic typicality, which isn't the same thing as being a word people use, and shipping it would have made the exact failure worse.

The real fix is word-frequency data, which means a new external dependency with licensing questions attached, so it isn't in there. The cheap fix is one line: drop today's answer list into data/answers.txt and rebuild. The cache keys hash the word list, so everything downstream regenerates itself.

Closing

Between the persistent book of solved positions and the mmap'd tables, a full game went from 11 seconds to 56 milliseconds. That felt like the point of the project while I was chasing it, and it wasn't.

The search was the easy half, and it was easy because someone had already proved what the answer was. The actual difficulty was all in the gap between the problem in the paper and the problem on my screen: a word list that had quietly changed underneath me, an infinity that destroyed information I needed, and a benchmark that agreed with a published proof while two bugs sat under it.

Optimal is a property of a model, and the model is the part nobody publishes a proof for.
